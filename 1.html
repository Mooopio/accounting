Definition of Accounting

A. Accounting as a System

Definition: Accounting is a system to provide quantitative information with financial nature about an economic entity to help in making economic decisions.

Think of accounting as the GPS of business. Just as GPS tells you where you are and how to reach your destination, accounting tells a business its financial position and guides decisions about the future.

The definition has three critical pillars:

  • Quantitative information — Accounting speaks in numbers. Non-numerical information (e.g., employee morale, office location, management style) is excluded. If you can't count it in money, accounting doesn't record it directly.
  • Financial nature — Even among numbers, only those expressible in monetary units qualify. A company hiring 50 workers is not an accounting event until salaries are paid or owed.
  • Decision making — Everything accounting does ultimately exists to help someone make a better economic decision. This is the final and most important purpose.

B. Accounting as a Process

Definition: A process of identifying, recording, summarizing, and reporting economic information about an economic entity to decision makers.

The four steps form a pipeline — each stage feeds the next:

① Identifying — What counts as an accounting event?▼

Analyzing every business event to decide: Is it financial? Can it be expressed in money? Non-financial events (hiring, training) are not recorded. A sale, a purchase, a loan — these are identified as accounting events.

Analogy: Like a customs officer deciding what goods to let into the country — only "qualifying items" pass through.

② Recording — Journalizing▼

Once identified, transactions are entered (journalized) into the journal — the book of original entry. This is done chronologically and is the first formal capture of every financial event.

③ Summarizing — Ledger & Trial Balance▼

Journal entries are transferred (posted) to the ledger — a book with one page per account. The ledger classifies all transactions by account type. The trial balance then checks that debits = credits before preparing statements.

④ Reporting — Financial Statements▼

The final output: general-purpose financial statements — the language of business communicated to users inside and outside the firm.

The Four External Financial Statements

1️⃣ Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet)▼

Contains permanent (real) accounts: Assets, Liabilities, and Owner's Equity. Shows what the company owns and what it owes at a specific date.

  • Assets = Expected economic benefits controlled by the entity as a result of past transactions (land, buildings, equipment, cash, inventory, accounts receivable, prepaid expenses, etc.)
  • Liabilities = Future economic sacrifices — obligations to pay or render service (accounts payable, loans, notes payable, unearned revenue, etc.)
  • Owner's Equity = Assets − Liabilities. Includes Capital + Net Profit − Drawings.
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
2️⃣ Income Statement▼

Prepared at the end of each accounting period (usually 1 year). Contains temporary (nominal) accounts: Revenues and Expenses.

  • Revenue = Price received for selling goods/services, regardless of whether cash was actually collected.
  • Expense = Cost of using resources to generate revenue.
  • Profit (Net Income) = Revenues > Expenses → increases equity.
  • Loss (as a result) = Revenues < Expenses → decreases equity.
  • Loss (as an item) = An expense from non-central, unrepeatable activities with no benefit in return.
  • Gain = Inflow from non-central, non-repeatable activities — increases equity.
Profit / Loss = Total Revenues − Total Expenses
3️⃣ Statement of Owners' Equity▼

Measures and calculates the company's obligations toward its owners. Shows changes in owners' equity over the period: starting capital + net income − drawings = ending equity.

4️⃣ Statement of Cash Flows▼

Shows all cash inflows (cash collected) and cash outflows (cash paid) during the period. Divided into operating, investing, and financing activities.

External User Groups (6 Groups)

👤 1. Investors (Current & Potential)▼
  • Current investors: Need info on cash flows and risk level to decide whether to continue investing or exit.
  • Potential (future) investors: Need the same info to compare alternatives and find the best investment opportunity.
🏦 2. Creditors and Banks▼

Need financial position information to evaluate the firm's ability to repay loans and debts.

🏭 3. Suppliers▼

Need to ensure the company will continue operating actively and has the ability to repay its obligations to them.

🛒 4. Customers▼

Need to confirm that goods and services will continue to be available — including spare parts, maintenance, and replacements.

👷 5. Manpower & Workers' Unions▼

Need info to know their share in profits, to support negotiations on salaries and benefits, and to evaluate the employment position.

⚔️ 6. Competitors▼

Need info to evaluate their own competitive position and the possibility of growth and expansion.

🏛️ Governmental Agencies & Controlling Organizations▼

Includes: Tax Authority, Ministry of Planning, Ministry of Industry, Stock Exchange Commission. Need info to evaluate entities' contribution to national income and added value, and to improve governmental performance.

📊 Financial Analysts & Public▼
  • Financial Analysts: Evaluate firm performance to provide investment consultation and support academic research.
  • Public: Know the services and products available in their area.

The 4 Characteristics of a Corporation

  1. Unlimited ability to raise funds — Can sell shares to the public to raise capital.
  2. Flexibility of capital transfer — Shareholders can sell their shares freely.
  3. Continuity — The corporation continues even if an owner dies or sells shares.
  4. Separate legal entity — The corporation is a legal "person" distinct from its owners — it can sue and be sued in its own name.

Common Exam Traps & Mistakes
Trap 1: "Accounting records all information about a company." ❌
✅ Accounting records ONLY quantitative, financial information expressed in monetary units.
Trap 2: "Revenue is only recognized when cash is collected." ❌
✅ Revenue is recognized when goods/services are provided, regardless of cash collection.
Trap 3: "A loss on the income statement means expenses exceed revenues." ⚠️
✅ "Loss as a result" = expenses > revenues. But "loss as an item" = a specific expense from non-central activities. These are DIFFERENT things.
Trap 4: "Financial accounting is for internal users." ❌
✅ Financial accounting is primarily for EXTERNAL users. Managerial accounting is for internal.
Trap 5: "In a partnership, the limit is 20 total owners." ✅ Correct — maximum 20 partners.
Trap 6: "Auditing is an internal accounting field." ❌
✅ External auditors are independent. They examine from outside and give an opinion on statement fairness.
Trap 7: "The auditor is not responsible if they miss something." ❌
✅ The auditor IS responsible for any negligence in their audit.
Trap 8: Confusing "permanent accounts" and "temporary accounts."
✅ Permanent (real) = Assets, Liabilities, OE (stay on balance sheet year after year).
✅ Temporary (nominal) = Revenues, Expenses (reset to zero each period).

Information Chunking

Chunk 1: The Big 3 Definitions

Asset = Expected economic benefit, controlled by entity, from past transactions.
Liability = Future economic sacrifice, from current obligations, from past transactions.
Owner's Equity = Assets minus Liabilities (residual interest of owners).

Chunk 2: Temporary vs. Permanent Accounts

Permanent = Balance Sheet items (Assets, Liabilities, OE) — carry forward.
Temporary = Income Statement items (Revenues, Expenses) — reset each period.

Chunk 3: The Accounting Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
OE = Capital + Net Profit − Drawings

Chunk 4: Two Main Types of Cost Systems

Actual Cost System — records costs as they actually occur.
Predetermined Cost System — sets expected costs in advance → then compares actual vs. predetermined → calculates variances → takes corrective action.
