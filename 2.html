1 — Balance Sheet Assumptions & Principles

4 fundamental assumptions that govern how B/S is prepared

🏢 Accounting Entity Assumption ▼

Definition: The business is treated as a separate legal person from its owner(s).

  • The entity owns assets and owes liabilities independently of the owner.
  • Owner's personal transactions are NEVER mixed with business transactions.
  • Think of it like this: if Ahmed owns a shop, the shop has its own wallet — Ahmed cannot dip into it for personal groceries without recording it.
🎯 Exam Tip
Any transaction between the owner and the business must be recorded. Withdrawals (Drawings) reduce owner's equity.
♾️ Going Concern Assumption ▼

Definition: The entity is assumed to be established to continue indefinitely — it will not be liquidated in the near future.

  • Justifies recording assets at historical cost (not liquidation value).
  • Allows for depreciation spread over an asset's useful life.
  • Analogy: It's like building a house assuming you'll live in it for years, not that you'll sell it tomorrow.
💡 Key Link
This assumption is WHY we use the cost principle — if the business were ending, we'd use market values instead.
💰 The Cost Principle (Historical Cost) ▼

Definition: Assets and liabilities are recorded and reported at their original purchase price (historical cost), NOT their current market value.

  • If a building was bought for $30,000 in 2020 but is now worth $60,000 — it still shows as $30,000 (minus depreciation).
  • This provides objectivity and verifiability — cost is a fact; market value is an opinion.
  • B/S amounts ≠ market values.
⚠️ Common Trap
Students confuse historical cost with current/fair value. In this course, ALWAYS use historical cost for B/S items.
💵 Stable Monetary Unit Assumption ▼

Definition: Assumes the monetary unit (dollar, EGP, etc.) has stable purchasing power over time.

  • In reality, this is NOT perfectly true — inflation erodes purchasing power.
  • Inflation → purchasing power DECREASES (money buys less).
  • Deflation → purchasing power INCREASES (money buys more).
  • But accounting ignores these changes and treats $1 from 1990 = $1 today.
📝 Real World
This assumption simplifies accounting but is one reason financial statements can be misleading during high inflation periods (like in Egypt).

2 — Income Statement Assumptions & Principles

How revenues and expenses are measured and matched

📅 Periodicity (Time Period) Assumption

Business life is divided into equal time periods (usually 1 year) to measure performance, rather than waiting until the business ends.

  • Periods can be: Monthly | Quarterly | Semi-annually | Annually (mandatory)
  • Enables timely decision-making for managers, investors, and creditors.
  • Analogy: Like a student who checks grades every semester, not just at graduation.
💹 Revenue Recognition Principle

Revenue is recorded WHEN EARNED, not when cash is received.

  • Revenue is realized/earned when: goods are delivered OR services are rendered.
  • Cash collection is IRRELEVANT to when revenue is recognized.
  • Example: If you perform a service in December but get paid in January → record revenue in December.
🎯 Exam Hotspot
Service on credit = Revenue recognized now (A/R is created). Cash collected later = No new revenue, just exchange assets.
⚖️ The Matching Principle

Expenses must be matched with the revenues they helped generate in the same period.

  • Net Income = Revenues − Expenses (for the same period).
  • If salaries were paid to generate December revenue, record the salaries in December even if paid in January.
  • This is why we have "Expenses Payable" (accrued expenses) on the B/S.

3 — The Accounting Equation

The backbone of double-entry bookkeeping

ASSETS = LIABILITIES + OWNER'S EQUITY
Understanding the Equation
Component What It Means Examples
Assets Everything the business OWNS (economic resources) Cash, Building, Equipment, Inventory, A/R, Prepaid Expenses
Liabilities Everything the business OWES to outsiders (creditors) Loans Payable, A/P, N/P, Unearned Revenue, Expenses Payable
Owner's Equity Owner's share = Capital + Income − Expenses − Drawings Capital, Net Income/Loss, Drawings
💡 The Golden Rule
Every business transaction affects the equation but ALWAYS leaves it balanced. If Assets increase, either Liabilities increase OR Owner's Equity increases (or both, in proportion).
Owner's Equity Formula
Owner's Equity = Capital
                   + Net Income (Revenue − Expenses)
                   + Additional Investments
                   − Drawings (withdrawals)
                   − Net Loss

17 Transaction Types — Detailed Analysis

Every standard transaction and its effect on the accounting equation

T1 — Owner Deposits Capital

Cash ↑ (Asset ↑) | Capital ↑ (OE ↑) — Equation balanced

Example: Ahmed deposits $50,000 → Cash +50,000 | Capital +50,000
T2 — Bank Loan

Cash ↑ (Asset ↑) | Loan Payable ↑ (Liability ↑) — both sides increase equally

Example: $25,000 loan → Cash +25,000 | Loans Payable +25,000
T3 — Purchase Asset for Cash

Cash ↓ (Asset ↓) | New Asset ↑ (Asset ↑) — Assets stay same; just swap form

Example: Building $30,000 cash → Cash -30,000 | Building +30,000
T4 — Purchase Asset on Credit (A/P)

New Asset ↑ (Asset ↑) | A/P ↑ (Liability ↑) — both sides increase

Example: Computer $5,000 on credit → Computer +5,000 | A/P +5,000
T5 — Partial Cash + Note Payable

Asset ↑ full price | Cash ↓ partial | N/P ↑ remainder

Example: Supplies $3,000: Cash -1,000, Supplies +3,000, N/P +2,000
T6 — Sell Asset at Cost for Cash

Cash ↑ | Old Asset ↓ — No gain/loss; just swap assets

Example: Sell half building $15,000 → Cash +15,000 | Building -15,000
T7 — Sell Asset on Credit (A/R)

A/R ↑ (new asset) | Old Asset ↓ — No cash involved yet

Example: Sell computer on credit $5,000 → A/R +5,000 | Computer -5,000
T8 — Repay Part of Loan

Cash ↓ (Asset ↓) | Loan Payable ↓ (Liability ↓) — both sides decrease

Example: Repay $10,000 → Cash -10,000 | L/P -10,000
T9 — Collect Accounts Receivable

Cash ↑ | A/R ↓ — Asset swap; no new revenue!

Example: Collect $5,000 → Cash +5,000 | A/R -5,000
T10 — Additional Owner Investment

Cash ↑ | Capital ↑ (OE ↑) — owner adds more money

T11 — Reduce Capital (Withdrawal to reduce investment)

Cash ↓ | Capital ↓ — permanent reduction in investment

T12 — Drawings (Personal Use)

Cash ↓ | Drawings ↑ (which reduces Capital/OE) — anticipation of profit

Distinction!
Drawings = temporary withdrawal expecting profit. Reducing Capital = permanent reduction of investment.
T13 — Service Revenue (Cash)

Cash ↑ | Revenue ↑ (OE ↑) — income earned and collected

Example: Trip arranged for $7,000 cash → Cash +7,000 | Revenue +7,000
T14 — Service Revenue (Credit)

A/R ↑ | Revenue ↑ (OE ↑) — income earned but not yet collected

Example: Trip to Luxor $5,000 on credit → A/R +5,000 | Revenue +5,000
T15 — Collect Revenue Previously on Credit

Cash ↑ | A/R ↓ — No new revenue! Just asset conversion

T16 — Pay Expense in Cash

Cash ↓ | Expense ↑ (reduces OE) — decreases owner's equity

Example: Salaries $8,000 → Cash -8,000 | Expenses -8,000 (OE)
T17 — Expense Not Yet Paid (Accrued)

Expenses Payable ↑ (Liability ↑) | Expense ↑ (reduces OE) — liability created

Example: Rent $2,000 unpaid → Exp. Payable +2,000 | Expenses -2,000 (OE)

4 — The Debit & Credit Rule

The universal rule for recording transactions

The T-Account Framework
ASSETS
Normal Balance: DEBIT
↑ Increase → Debit
↓ Decrease → Credit
LIABILITIES & OWNER'S EQUITY
Normal Balance: CREDIT
↑ Increase → Credit
↓ Decrease → Debit
💡 The Core Insight
Debit = Left side of T-account. Credit = Right side. For EVERY entry: Total Debits MUST EQUAL Total Credits.
Sub-rules for Owner's Equity Components
AccountIncreases withDecreases withNormal Balance
RevenueCreditDebitCredit
ExpensesDebitCreditDebit
DrawingsDebitCreditDebit
CapitalCreditDebitCredit
📝 Why expenses debit?
Expenses REDUCE owner's equity. OE has a credit normal balance, so to reduce it you DEBIT it. Expenses, drawings = debit because they fight against OE.

Smart Summary — High-Yield Points

Everything you need, nothing you don't

🏗️ B/S Assumptions (4)
  • 1. Entity Assumption — Business is a separate legal person from owner. Personal ≠ Business.
  • 2. Going Concern — Business is assumed to continue indefinitely. Life is continuous, not temporary.
  • 3. Cost Principle — Record at historical/original cost, NOT market value. B/S ≠ current market values.
  • 4. Stable Monetary Unit — Assume purchasing power of currency is fixed. (Not perfectly true in reality.)
📊 I/S Assumptions (3)
  • 1. Periodicity — Business life divided into equal periods (usually 1 year). Enables periodic reporting.
  • 2. Revenue Recognition — Record revenue when EARNED (service rendered/goods delivered), not when cash received.
  • 3. Matching Principle — Match expenses to the revenues they generated in the SAME period.
⚖️ The Accounting Equation
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
  • • Always balanced after EVERY transaction
  • • Assets: Cash, Building, Equipment, Supplies, A/R, Inventory, Prepaid Exp.
  • • Liabilities: L/P, A/P, N/P, Unearned Revenue, Expenses Payable, Bonds Payable
  • • OE = Capital + Revenue − Expenses − Drawings + Additional Investment − Net Loss

🔴 Common Exam Mistakes & Traps
TRAP 1:

Collecting A/R creates NEW revenue. ❌ WRONG! Revenue was already recorded when earned. Collecting A/R just converts one asset to another (A/R → Cash).

TRAP 2:

B/S shows current market value of assets. ❌ WRONG! B/S shows historical cost (cost principle). Market values are NOT used.

TRAP 3:

Revenue = when cash is received. ❌ WRONG! Revenue recognition = when service is rendered or goods delivered, regardless of payment.

TRAP 4:

Drawings = Expense. ❌ WRONG! Drawings are NOT an expense. They are a direct reduction of owner's equity/capital.

TRAP 5:

Buying asset for cash increases total assets. ❌ WRONG! Cash decreases (one asset), new asset increases → net change = ZERO. Total assets unchanged.

TRAP 6:

Loans payable is part of owner's equity. ❌ WRONG! Loans payable is a LIABILITY — money owed to creditors, not the owner.

Key Transaction Rules

  • Buy asset cash → Asset swap, OE unchanged
  • Buy on credit → Asset↑ + Liability↑
  • Revenue cash → Cash↑ + OE↑
  • Revenue credit → A/R↑ + OE↑
  • Collect A/R → Cash↑ + A/R↓ (no new revenue!)
  • Pay expense → Cash↓ + OE↓

Watch Out For

  • A/R collection ≠ new revenue
  • B/S ≠ market values
  • Revenue when earned, not paid
  • Drawings ≠ Expense
  • Loans = Liability (not OE)
  • Buy asset cash = no OE change
